\section{Symbols}

\noindent Looking at profitability:

\begin{tabular}{ll}
  $\eta$    & overall profit-on-investment \\
  $i$       & capital bound into an investment (on day zero) \\
  $d$       & days an investment $i$ is bound (also: days a train needs for a run) \\
  $p$       & profit for an investment $i$ after $d$ days \\
  $y$       & yield for a delivery
\end{tabular}

\noindent Looking at assets:

\begin{tabular}{ll}
  $e$    & earnings (also: fictional cost) \\
  $m$    & maintenance cost \\
  $l$    & track length \\
  $o$    & occupation
\end{tabular}


\section{Concepts}


\subsection{Profit-on-investment for a single investment}

The profit-on-investment per week $\eta_w$ is:

\begin{equation}
  \eta_w = \left(\frac{p}{i}+1\right)^{\left(7/d\right)}-1 \label{eq:weekly_profit}
\end{equation}

Conversely, the weekly profit $p_w$ for a given investment $i$ is:

\begin{equation}
  p_w = i \cdot \eta_w
\end{equation}

Therefore, the mean profitability $\overline\eta_w$ of multiple investmens $i_n$ with
separate profitabilities $\eta_{w,n}$ is just the average weighted by investments:

\begin{equation}
  \overline\eta_w = \frac{\sum_n i_n \cdot \eta_{w,n}}{\sum_n i_n}
\end{equation}




\subsection{One-shot line}
\label{sec:one_shot_line}

For a one-shot line, two terminal stations (between producer and consumer),
a track (of some cost and length), and a train is setup for a period of
$d$ days, and sold afterwards. The profit $p$ is the yield $y$ minus any
maintenance and deprecation costs.

A one-shot line is considered to have no shared assets and hence no associated cost splitting
and no profit splitting; all investments are made at day zero and are again liquidated at day $d$.

Overall investment and profit:

\begin{eqnarray}
  i &=& i\left(\mathrm{station1}\right)
     +  i\left(\mathrm{station2}\right)
     +  i\left(\mathrm{track}\right)
     +  i_\mathit{train} \\
  p &=& y
     -  m\left(\mathrm{train}\right)
\end{eqnarray}

(Since investment happens at day zero, the maintenance cost accumulated at day $d$ is deducted
from the profit at that day, instead of being added to the upfront investment.)

With these $i$ and $p$, the efficiency $\eta_w$ can be computed using eq.~\ref{eq:weekly_profit}.


\subsection{Train running on shared assets}

When assets are shared then instead of calculating based on their investment, we calculate based on
some fictional cost (earnings) $e$ for them. The fictional costs are the earnings $e$ contributed to each asset.

For the one-shot line from sec.~\ref{sec:one_shot_line}, this becomes:

\begin{eqnarray}
  i &=& i_\mathit{train} \\
  p &=& p_\mathit{train} \nonumber \\
    &=& y_\mathit{train} \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot \biggl(
          e_w\left(\mathrm{station1}\right)
       +  e_w\left(\mathrm{station2}\right)
       +  e_w\left(\mathrm{track}\right)
       \biggr) \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot m_{w,\mathit{train}}
\end{eqnarray}

\subsubsection{Fictional infrastructure costs}

There is no real value for the fictional costs $e_w\left(\mathrm{station}\right)$ and $e_w\left(\mathrm{track}\right)$,
we have to come up with an estimate that is overall consistent.

Obviously, longer tracks are less efficient, so earning should be split not by count $e_w\left(\mathrm{track}\right)$
but by miles $l \cdot e_w\left(\mathrm{mile}\right)$:

\begin{eqnarray}
  p_\mathit{train}
    &=& y_\mathit{train} \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot e_w\left(\mathrm{station1}\right) \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot e_w\left(\mathrm{station2}\right) \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot l\left(\mathrm{track}\right) \cdot e_w\left(\mathrm{mile}\right) \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot m_{w,\mathit{train}}
\end{eqnarray}

Regarding track miles, note that there are single and double tracks. For double tracks, $l$ will
be twice as much as for single tracks, which allows to keep $e_w\left(\mathrm{mile}\right)$ the same.

Next, assets like stations and tracks are shared by one or more trains, so the earning of each of these
should be fairly shared by the trains.

We count the occupation by the period $d$ of each train.

\begin{eqnarray}
  o\left(\mathrm{station}\right) &=& \sum_{\mathrm{trains@station}} d_{\mathrm{train}} \\
  o\left(\mathrm{track}\right)   &=& \sum_{\mathrm{trains@track}} d_{\mathrm{train}}
\end{eqnarray}

Again, this penalizes long-running trains over short-running ones and satisfies the requirement that
all required infrastructure must be present at all times during $d_{\mathrm{train}}$.

With that we get:

\begin{eqnarray}
  p\left(\mathrm{train}\right)
    &=& y\left(\mathrm{train}\right) \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot \frac{d_\mathrm{train}}{o_\mathrm{station1}} \cdot e_w\left(\mathrm{station1}\right) \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot \frac{d_\mathrm{train}}{o_\mathrm{station2}} \cdot e_w\left(\mathrm{station2}\right) \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot \frac{d_\mathrm{train}}{o_\mathrm{track}} \cdot l \cdot e_w\left(\mathrm{mile}\right) \nonumber \\
    &-& \frac{d}{7} \cdot m_{w,\mathit{train}}
\end{eqnarray}

With that we also get:

\begin{eqnarray}
  p_w\left(\mathrm{station}\right) &=& e_w\left(\mathrm{station}\right) \\
  p_w\left(\mathrm{track}\right)   &=& e_w\left(\mathrm{track}\right)
\end{eqnarray}

Again, we have to invest for the infrastructure, so given $i$ and $p$ of infrastructure items, we can compute their profitability $\eta_w$,
and use that to find $e_w\left(\mathrm{station}\right)$ and $e_w\left(\mathrm{track}\right)$.

\subsubsection{Fictional infrastructure costs from contrived one-shot line example}

Let's assume:

\begin{eqnarray}
i\left(\mathrm{station}\right) &=& \$\, 40'000 \\
d_\mathit{load}\left(\mathrm{station}\right) &=& 10 \,\mathrm{days} \\
d_\mathit{unload}\left(\mathrm{station}\right)  &=& 10 \,\mathrm{days} \\
i\left(\mathrm{track}\right)   &=& \$\, 40'000 \\
l\left(\mathrm{track}\right)   &=& 50 \,\mathrm{miles} \\
i_\mathit{train}   &=& \$\, 40000 \\
y_\mathit{train}   &=& \$\, 16000 \\
m_\mathit{train}   &=& \$\, 2 /\mathrm{day}\\
v_\mathit{train}   &=& 0.5 \,\mathrm{miles}/\mathrm{day}
\end{eqnarray}

Intermediate values are:

\begin{eqnarray}
d_\mathit{train}    &=& d_\mathit{load}\left(\mathrm{station1}\right) + d_\mathit{unload}\left(\mathrm{station2}\right) + \frac{l\left(\mathrm{track}\right)}{v_\mathit{train}} \\
w_\mathit{train}    &=& d_\mathit{train} / 7
\end{eqnarray}


The equations for the profitabilities of each part are:

\begin{eqnarray}
\eta_{w,\mathit{s1}} &=& e_{w,\mathit{s1}}
                                         / i\left(\mathrm{station1}\right) \\
\eta_{w,\mathit{s2}} &=& e_{w,\mathit{s2}}
                                         / i\left(\mathrm{station2}\right) \\
\eta_{w,\mathrm{mile}} &=& e_{w,\mathit{mile}}
                                         / i\left(\mathrm{mile}\right) \\
\eta_{w,\mathit{train}} + 1  
  &=& 
    \left( 
      \frac{ 
        y_\mathit{train} 
        - w_\mathit{train} \cdot \left( \begin{array}{c} 
          e_{w,\mathit{s1}} \\ 
          + e_{w,\mathit{s2}} \\ 
          + l_\mathit{track} e_{w,\mathit{mile}} \\ 
          + 7  m_\mathit{train} 
        \end{array} \right)
      }{ i_\mathit{train} } +1 
    \right) ^{w_\mathit{train}}
\end{eqnarray}


The unknowns are the the fictional weekly costs $e_{w,\mathit{s1}}$, $e_{w,\mathit{s2}}$, $e_{w,\mathit{mile}}$.

The solution is that where all profitabilities are balanced,

\begin{equation}
\eta_w = \eta_{w,\mathit{s1}} = \eta_{w,\mathit{s2}} = \eta_{w,\mathrm{mile}} = \eta_{w,\mathit{train}}
\end{equation}

So we can collapse the system of equations to:

\begin{eqnarray}
\eta_w + 1  
  &=& 
    \left( 
      \frac{ 
        y_\mathit{train} 
        - w_\mathit{train} \cdot \left( \begin{array}{c} 
          \eta_w \cdot i_\mathit{s1} \\ 
          + \eta_w \cdot i_\mathit{s2} \\ 
          + \eta_w \cdot i_\mathit{mile} \cdot l_\mathit{track} \\ 
          + 7  m_\mathit{train} 
        \end{array} \right)
      }{ i_\mathit{train} } +1 
    \right) ^{w_\mathit{train}} \\
  &=& 
    \left( 
      \frac{ 
        \left( \begin{array}{c} 
          y_\mathit{train} \\
          - 7 \, w_\mathit{train} \, m_\mathit{train} 
        \end{array} \right)    
        - \eta_w \, w_\mathit{train} \, \left( \begin{array}{c} 
          i_\mathit{s1} + i_\mathit{s2} \\ 
          + i_\mathit{mile} \cdot l_\mathit{track}
        \end{array} \right)
      }{ i_\mathit{train} } +1 
    \right) ^{w_\mathit{train}}
\end{eqnarray}

The basic structure of the problem to solve is:

\begin{eqnarray}
\eta_w + 1  
  &=& 
    \left( 
      c_1 - \eta_w \, c_2 + 1 
    \right) ^{c_3} \label{equ:eta_w_1} \\
c_1 &=& \frac{y_\mathit{train} - 7 \, w_\mathit{train} \, m_\mathit{train}}{i_\mathit{train}} \label{equ:c1} \\
c_2 &=& \frac{w_\mathit{train}}{i_\mathit{train}} \, \left( 
          i_\mathit{s1} + i_\mathit{s2} 
          + i_\mathit{mile} \cdot l_\mathit{track}
        \right) \\
c_3 &=& w_\mathit{train}
\end{eqnarray}

\subsubsection{Minimum train profitability and maximum train track length}

We requires that a train should at least pay for itself, so $c_1$ from eq.~\ref{equ:c1} should be positive. This puts a constraint on the travel duration $w_\mathit{train}$ and track length $l_\mathit{train}$ (ignoring the time for loading and unloading at stations):

\begin{eqnarray}
  c_1 > 0 
& \Longleftrightarrow &
  w_\mathit{train} < \frac{y_\mathit{train}}{7 \, m_\mathit{train}} \\
& \Longleftrightarrow &
  l_\mathit{train} < \frac{v_\mathit{train} \, y_\mathit{train}}{m_\mathit{train}}
\end{eqnarray}

(Using $l_\mathit{train}$ instead of $l_\mathit{track}$ on purpose.)

Since the latter boundary seems to be both handy and relevant, let's derive a non-dimensional value $\lambda_\mathit{train}$ from it:

\begin{eqnarray}
  \lambda_\mathit{train} &=& \frac{v_\mathit{train} \, y_\mathit{train}}{l_\mathit{train} \, m_\mathit{train} }
\end{eqnarray}

For $\lambda_\mathit{train}$ the minimum profitability constraint becomes:

\begin{eqnarray}
  c_1 > 0 & \Longleftrightarrow & \lambda_\mathit{train} > 1  
\end{eqnarray}

During the game, values for $y_\mathit{train}$, $v_\mathit{train}$ and $m_\mathit{train}$ change slowly by some percent only (with technical inventions or especially skilled employees), while the value for $l_\mathit{train}$ will vary by an order of magnitude at any time (connecting nearby stations versus connecting stations far away). Therefore, the value of $\lambda_\mathit{train}$ will be critical near $\lambda_\mathit{train} \approx 1$, when $l_\mathit{train}$ and consequently $w_\mathit{train}$ become large. In these cases, in turn, the times spend on loading and unloading become negligible, which justifies the simplification that the constraints $c_1 > 0$ and $\lambda_\mathit{train} > 1$ ignore them.

\subsubsection{Estimate fictional infrastructure costs from contrived one-shot line example}

For the region of interest, eq.~\ref{equ:eta_w_1} can be converted into a fixpoint equation for $\eta_w$ around $\eta_w=0$:

\begin{eqnarray}
\eta_{w,i+1} &=& \frac{1}{c_2}\,\left( 1 + c_1 - \left(1 + \eta_{w,i}\right)^{1/c_3}\right)
\end{eqnarray}

The two-iterations estimate is:

\begin{eqnarray}
\eta_{w,0} &=& 0 \\
\eta_{w,1} &=& c_1 / c_2 \\
\eta_{w,2} &=& \frac{1}{c_2}\,\left( 1 + c_1 - \left(1 + \frac{c_1}{c_2}\right)^{1/c_3}\right)
\end{eqnarray}

The approximate fictional infrastructure costs then are:

\begin{eqnarray}
e_{w,s} &\approx & \eta_{w,2} \, i_s \\
e_{w,\mathit{mile}} &\approx & \eta_{w,2} \, i_\mathit{mile}
\end{eqnarray}
